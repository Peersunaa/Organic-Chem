Structure and Bonding; Acids and Bases

Book pages 1–37 · §1.1–1.12
Why this matters: every reaction later in the course is electrons moving between atoms. This chapter teaches where electrons sit (orbitals), how atoms share them (bonds), what shape that makes (hybridization), and who pulls them harder (electronegativity, acids and bases).

§1.1–1.2Atoms and electron configurations

An atom is a tiny positive nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons. Electrons live in orbitals, regions where you are likely to find them. Orbitals are grouped into shells: shell 1 holds 2 electrons, shell 2 holds 8, shell 3 holds 18.

  • s orbitals are spheres. p orbitals are dumbbells, and there are three of them (px, py, pz) at right angles.
  • Fill electrons from lowest energy up (1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p). Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons with opposite spins. In equal-energy orbitals, put one electron in each before pairing.
  • Carbon (6 electrons): 1s2 2s2 2p2. It has 4 valence (outer-shell) electrons, which is why it makes 4 bonds.

§1.3–1.4How atoms bond

Atoms bond to reach a full outer shell (an octet, 8 electrons; hydrogen wants 2). Ionic bonds come from transferring electrons (Na+Cl−). Covalent bonds come from sharing a pair of electrons, and organic molecules are almost all covalent.

"HONC 1-2-3-4": hydrogen 1 bond, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4. If a neutral atom has a different count, re-check your structure.

Ways to draw molecules: electron-dot (Lewis) structures show every valence electron as a dot; line-bond (Kekulé) structures show each shared pair as a line. Electrons that are not in bonds are lone pairs.

§1.5–1.8Valence bond theory and hybrid orbitals

A covalent bond forms when orbitals on two atoms overlap and share two electrons. There are two kinds of overlap:

  • Sigma (σ) bond: head-on overlap along the line between the nuclei. Every single bond is σ. You can rotate around it freely.
  • Pi (π) bond: sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below the bond axis. A double bond = 1 σ + 1 π. A triple bond = 1 σ + 2 π. You cannot rotate around a π bond without breaking it.

Carbon mixes its 2s and 2p orbitals into hybrid orbitals so its bonds point as far apart as possible.

Count the "electron groups" on the atom: each attached atom counts 1, and each lone pair counts 1. Double and triple bonds count as one group. 4 groups → sp3, 3 → sp2, 2 → sp. This also works for N and O: NH3 (3 atoms + 1 lone pair) is sp3 with angles of 107°; H2O (2 + 2) is sp3 with 104.5°. Lone pairs squeeze the angles below 109.5°.

More bonds between two carbons means shorter and stronger: triple > double > single.

What is the hybridization of each carbon in CH2=CH–C≡N?

C1 (=CH2) is sp2, C2 (=CH–) is sp2, and C3 (–C≡N) is sp. The N has a triple bond and a lone pair, so it is sp too.

§1.9Polar covalent bonds: electronegativity

Electronegativity (EN) is how strongly an atom pulls on the electrons in a bond. When two atoms with different EN share electrons, the more electronegative one gets a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other a partial positive charge (δ+). This is a polar covalent bond.

  • EN difference under about 0.5: essentially nonpolar (C–H, C–C).
  • Between about 0.5 and 2: polar covalent (C–O, C–Cl, O–H).
  • Over about 2: ionic (Na+Cl−).

The inductive effect is an atom shifting electrons toward itself through σ bonds. Electronegative atoms (O, N, halogens) pull electrons away from carbon, making that carbon δ+. Metals do the opposite: in a C–Mg or C–Li bond, carbon is δ−.

Students often forget that carbon is δ− when bonded to a metal. In CH3–Li the carbon is electron-rich. In CH3–Cl it is electron-poor.

§1.10Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton (H+). A base accepts a proton. When an acid loses H+, what remains is its conjugate base; when a base gains H+, it becomes its conjugate acid.

HA + H2O ⇌ A− + H3O+    Ka = [A−][H3O+] / [HA]    pKa = −log Ka
Smaller pKa = stronger acid. The stronger the acid, the weaker (more stable) its conjugate base. An acid–base equilibrium always favors the side with the weaker acid (the one with the higher pKa).
HCN + OH− ⇌ CN− + H2O. Which side is favored?

The right. Compare the two acids: HCN (pKa 9.31) and H2O (pKa 15.74). Water is the weaker acid, so the equilibrium lies on the water side. Hydroxide is a strong enough base to deprotonate HCN.

§1.11Organic acids and organic bases

  • Organic acids have a hydrogen on an electronegative atom (O–H in alcohols and carboxylic acids), or a C–H next to a C=O (acetone, pKa 19.3).
  • Carboxylic acids (pKa about 4–5) are much more acidic than alcohols (about 16). Their conjugate base, the carboxylate ion, spreads its negative charge over two oxygens, which makes it stable.
  • Organic bases have a lone pair that can grab H+. Most are nitrogen compounds (amines like CH3NH2). Oxygen compounds (alcohols, ethers, ketones) can also act as weak bases toward strong acids.

§1.12Lewis acids and bases

The Lewis definition is broader and is the one that matters for mechanisms. A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair; a Lewis base donates an electron pair.

  • Lewis acids have an empty orbital or a positive charge: H+, BF3, AlCl3, FeBr3, ZnCl2, metal cations (Mg2+).
  • Lewis bases have a lone pair: H2O, alcohols, ethers, amines, carbonyl oxygens.
  • A curved arrow always starts at the electrons (the base) and points to where they go (the acid).
The curved arrow never starts at H+ or at the Lewis acid. It starts at the lone pair or bond that moves.

§2.2Alkanes, alkyl groups and isomers

Alkanes contain only single bonds. They are saturated hydrocarbons with formula CnH2n+2. Isomers have the same formula but different structures. Constitutional isomers differ in which atoms are connected to which (butane vs isobutane, both C4H10).

Classify carbons by how many other carbons they're bonded to: primary (1°) = 1, secondary (2°) = 2, tertiary (3°) = 3, quaternary (4°) = 4. Hydrogens take the label of the carbon they're on.

§2.3Naming branched alkanes (IUPAC)

  1. Find the longest chain. That's the parent name. If two chains tie, choose the one with more branches.
  2. Number the chain from the end nearer the first branch. If it's a tie, go to the next branch.
  3. Name and number each substituent. Two identical groups on the same carbon each get the number (2,2-dimethyl).
  4. Write it as one word, substituents in alphabetical order. Ignore di-, tri-, sec-, tert- when alphabetizing. Do alphabetize "iso" (isopropyl goes under i).
  5. Commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and letters.
CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH(CH2CH3)CH2CH3 → 4-ethyl-2-methylhexane
The longest chain is often bent in the drawing. Trace every path. "2-ethylpentane" is never a correct name, because the ethyl group makes a longer chain (it's 3-methylhexane).
Name (CH3)3CCH2CH(CH3)2.

2,2,4-Trimethylpentane (isooctane). The main chain has 5 carbons. Numbering from the left gives 2,2,4; from the right gives 2,4,4. Choose the lower set: 2,2,4.

§2.4Properties of alkanes

  • Nonpolar and unreactive ("paraffins" means little affinity). They burn (combustion to CO2 + H2O) and react with Cl2 in light.
  • Boiling and melting points rise with chain length, because bigger molecules have stronger dispersion forces.
  • Branching lowers the boiling point: a more compact, rounder molecule has less surface contact. Pentane boils at 36 °C; 2,2-dimethylpropane at 9.5 °C.

§2.5Conformations of ethane

Because a σ bond is symmetric, groups can rotate around it. Each different rotational arrangement is a conformation (conformer). A Newman projection looks straight down the C–C bond: the front carbon is the point where three bonds meet, and the back carbon is the circle.

The barrier to rotation in ethane is 12 kJ/mol, which is 4.0 kJ/mol for each of the three eclipsed H–H pairs. That's small enough that rotation happens millions of times per second at room temperature.

§2.6Drawing skeletal structures

  • Carbons aren't written. A carbon sits at every corner and at every line end.
  • Hydrogens on carbon aren't drawn. Fill them in so each carbon has 4 bonds.
  • All other atoms (O, N, Cl…) are written, along with any H on them (–OH, –NH2).
To count hydrogens on a corner: 4 minus the number of lines going into it. A corner with two lines is CH2; a line end is CH3.

§2.7–2.8Cycloalkanes and cis–trans isomers

Cycloalkanes are rings, formula CnH2n. Name them with "cyclo-" (cyclohexane). With one substituent, no number is needed (methylcyclohexane). With more, number the ring to give the lowest numbers, starting with the group that comes first alphabetically. If the chain has more carbons than the ring, the ring becomes the substituent ("cyclopropyl").

Rings can't rotate freely, so two substituents can be on the same side of the ring (cis) or on opposite sides (trans). These are stereoisomers: the same connections but a different 3D arrangement. They can't interconvert without breaking bonds.

§2.9–2.11Cyclohexane: chair, axial and equatorial, ring-flip

Small rings are strained. Cyclopropane has 60° angles (angle strain) and eclipsed hydrogens. Cyclohexane avoids both by puckering into a chair conformation: all angles are about 111° and every C–H bond is staggered. It is strain-free, which is why six-membered rings are everywhere in nature.

  • Ring-flip: the chair flips into another chair. Every axial position becomes equatorial and vice versa, but "up" stays "up".
  • Substituents prefer equatorial. An axial group bumps into the two axial hydrogens three carbons away (1,3-diaxial interactions, a kind of steric strain).
  • Methylcyclohexane: equatorial is more stable by 7.6 kJ/mol, so about 95% of molecules have the methyl equatorial. Bigger groups (tert-butyl) are almost 100% equatorial.

Alkenes and Alkynes: The Nature of Organic Reactions

Book pages 78–111 · §3.1–3.9
Why this matters: this chapter gives you the language of reactions (nucleophile, electrophile, curved arrows, energy diagrams) that Chapters 4 and 5 use on every page. E/Z naming uses the same priority rules as R/S in Chapter 6.

§3.1Naming alkenes and alkynes

  1. The parent chain must contain the double (or triple) bond. Ending: -ene or -yne.
  2. Number from the end nearer the multiple bond, and give the bond the lower number of its two carbons.
  3. Number substituents and write the full name. Your book uses the newer IUPAC style, with the number just before the ending: but-2-ene, buta-1,3-diene, pent-1-yne.
  4. In cycloalkenes, the double bond is C1=C2. Number so the substituents get the lowest numbers.

Common names to know: ethylene (ethene), propylene (propene), acetylene (ethyne), vinyl (H2C=CH–), allyl (H2C=CHCH2–).

Alkenes and alkynes have fewer hydrogens than the matching alkane, so they are called unsaturated.

§3.2–3.3Double-bond structure and cis–trans isomers

A C=C is one σ bond (sp2–sp2) plus one π bond (p–p). To rotate, the π bond would have to break, which costs about 350 kJ/mol. So rotation doesn't happen at room temperature, and substituted alkenes can be cis (same side) or trans (opposite sides).

Cis–trans isomers only exist if each double-bond carbon has two different groups. CH2=CHCH3 (propene) has none, because C1 carries two H's.

Trans alkenes are usually more stable than cis, because in cis the bulky groups bump into each other.

§3.4Sequence rules: the E,Z system

When there are three or four different groups, "cis/trans" is ambiguous. Instead, rank the two groups on each carbon using the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog sequence rules:

  1. Higher atomic number wins for the atom directly attached: Br > Cl > O > N > C > H.
  2. Tie? Move outward. List the three atoms attached to each tied atom, highest first, and compare at the first point of difference. –CH2OH (O,H,H) beats –CH(CH3)2 (C,C,H), because O beats C.
  3. Multiple bonds count as duplicates. A C=O carbon counts as having (O,O,…); a –CH=CH2 counts as (C,C,H).
Higher-priority groups on the same side → Z (zusammen, "together"). On opposite sides → E (entgegen, "opposite"). Memory hook: Z = "zame zide".
Rule 2 compares the set of attached atoms at the first point of difference. It is not a sum. –C(CH3)3 (C,C,C) loses to –CH2Br (Br,H,H), because Br beats C at the first comparison.

§3.6How reactions occur: mechanisms

A mechanism describes step by step which bonds break and form. Bonds can break in two ways:

  • Homolytic (symmetric): each atom keeps one electron, making radicals. Shown with half-headed "fishhook" arrows. These are radical reactions, the less common kind.
  • Heterolytic (unsymmetric): one atom keeps both electrons, making ions. These are polar reactions, the most common kind.
Nucleophile ("nucleus-lover"): electron-rich, donates a pair. Has a lone pair, a π bond or a negative charge. Examples: OH−, Br−, H2O, NH3, C=C. It is a Lewis base.
Electrophile ("electron-lover"): electron-poor, accepts a pair. Has a + or δ+ atom or an empty orbital. Examples: H+, HBr, carbocations, BF3. It is a Lewis acid.
Curved-arrow rules: (1) the arrow starts at electrons (a lone pair or a bond) and ends at an atom. (2) A nucleophile attacks an electrophile; the arrow goes from the nucleophile to the electrophile. (3) Total charge is the same on both sides. (4) Don't give any atom more than an octet (2 for H).

§3.8Energy diagrams: transition states and intermediates

Transition state: the energy peak of a step. Bonds are half-formed and half-broken. It can't be isolated, because it exists for about one bond vibration.
Intermediate: an energy valley between two transition states. It is a real species (like a carbocation), but usually too reactive to bottle.
  • Activation energy (Eact): the climb from reactants to the transition state. Bigger Eact means a slower reaction. Around 40–150 kJ/mol is typical.
  • ΔH (heat of reaction): negative = exothermic (releases heat); positive = endothermic.
  • ΔH tells you whether a reaction is favorable. Eact tells you how fast it is. These are independent.

§3.9Catalysis

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. It provides a different pathway with a lower Eact. It does not change ΔH or where the equilibrium lies; it only gets you there faster. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

Reactions of Alkenes and Alkynes

Book pages 112–154 · §4.1–4.11
Why this matters: this is the first big "reaction list" chapter, and it carries the most exam points. For each reaction, know three things: the reagent, where things add (regiochemistry), and which side they add from (stereochemistry). The cheat sheet tab collects all of them.

§4.1Adding HX: Markovnikov's rule

HCl, HBr and HI add across a C=C. When the alkene is unsymmetrical, one product dominates. The reaction is regiospecific.

Markovnikov's rule: the H adds to the carbon that already has more H's (fewer alkyl groups), and the X goes to the carbon with more alkyl groups. The real reason is that this route goes through the more stable carbocation.
(CH3)2C=CH2 + HBr → (CH3)3C–Br   (2-bromo-2-methylpropane, via a 3° carbocation)
If both double-bond carbons have the same number of alkyl groups (like pent-2-ene), you get a mixture of both products. Markovnikov's rule doesn't pick one.

§4.2Carbocation structure and stability

A carbocation carbon is sp2 and flat, with an empty p orbital sticking up and down. Alkyl groups push electron density toward it (inductive effect), which stabilizes the positive charge. More alkyl groups means a more stable cation.

3° (R3C+)  >  2° (R2CH+)  >  1° (RCH2+)  >  methyl (CH3+)

Allylic (and benzylic) cations are extra stable because of resonance (§4.9).

§4.3Adding water: hydration

Alkene + H2O with an acid catalyst (H2SO4 or H3O+) → alcohol, Markovnikov orientation (OH on the more substituted carbon).

  1. The π bond takes H+ from H3O+, making the more stable carbocation.
  2. Water (nucleophile) attacks C+, giving a protonated alcohol (an oxonium ion).
  3. Water removes H+ from the oxygen, giving the alcohol. The acid catalyst is regenerated.
(CH3)2C=CH2 + H2O —H3O+→ (CH3)3C–OH  (tert-butyl alcohol)

§4.4Adding halogens: anti addition

Br2 or Cl2 in CH2Cl2 adds across the C=C to give a 1,2-dihalide. The bromine adds to opposite faces (anti stereochemistry).

  1. The π bond attacks Br2, forming a three-membered bromonium ion (Br+ bridging both carbons) and Br−.
  2. The bromonium ion blocks one face, so Br− attacks from the opposite side and opens the ring.
cyclopentene + Br2 → trans-1,2-dibromocyclopentane (not cis)
Br2 is red-brown. The color disappearing is a quick test for a C=C.

§4.5Reduction: hydrogenation

H2 with a metal catalyst (Pd on carbon, or PtO2) turns an alkene into an alkane. Both H's add from the same face (syn stereochemistry), because the alkene lies flat on the metal surface where the H atoms are bound.

In organic chemistry, reduction means gaining H or losing O (or N, X). Oxidation means gaining O (or N, X) or losing H.

What does acidic KMnO4 do to (CH3)2C=CHCH3?

It splits the double bond. The (CH3)2C= half becomes acetone, (CH3)2C=O. The =CHCH3 half has an H, so it becomes acetic acid, CH3COOH.

§4.7Radical addition: polymers

A polymer is a giant molecule made of repeating monomer units. Alkenes polymerize through a radical chain reaction started by a radical initiator such as benzoyl peroxide.

  1. Initiation: heat splits the initiator into radicals (In•), and In• adds to a monomer's C=C.
  2. Propagation: the new carbon radical adds to another monomer, over and over, so the chain grows.
  3. Termination: two radicals combine, and the chain stops.

Examples: ethylene → polyethylene; propylene → polypropylene; chloroethylene (vinyl chloride) → PVC; styrene → polystyrene; tetrafluoroethylene → Teflon.

§4.8–4.9Conjugated dienes and allylic carbocations

A conjugated diene has alternating double and single bonds (buta-1,3-diene). Its four p orbitals line up and overlap across the whole system.

HBr adds to buta-1,3-diene to give two products. In 1,2-addition, H and Br add to neighboring carbons. In 1,4-addition, they add to the ends, and the remaining double bond moves to the middle (C2=C3).

Why two products? Protonation at C1 gives an allylic carbocation, a cation next to a C=C. Its positive charge is shared between C2 and C4 by resonance. Br− can attack either carbon.

§4.10Drawing and interpreting resonance forms

  • Resonance forms are imaginary. The real molecule is a resonance hybrid, a blend of all forms, and it is more stable than any single form.
  • Only electrons move (π bonds and lone pairs). Atoms never move, and σ bonds stay put.
  • Each form must be a valid Lewis structure: no carbon with 5 bonds. The overall charge stays the same.
  • Typical pattern: three atoms, where one has a p orbital (a C=C next to a C+, a lone pair, or a radical). Use a double-headed arrow ↔ between forms.
  • More resonance forms means a more stable species.
If you moved a hydrogen or any atom, you drew a different molecule (an isomer), not a resonance form.

Aromatic Compounds

Book pages 155–188 · §5.1–5.10
Why this matters: benzene looks like it has three double bonds, but it doesn't react like an alkene. It does substitution instead of addition. Exams love multistep synthesis questions where the order of steps decides the product.

§5.1Structure of benzene

  • C6H6: flat, all angles 120°, every carbon sp2.
  • All six C–C bonds are the same length (139 pm), between a single bond (154) and a double bond (134).
  • It is unusually stable, so it resists addition. With Br2 it needs a catalyst, and then it substitutes an H instead of adding.
  • "Aromatic" today means having this special 6-π-electron ring stability, not having a smell.

§5.3–5.5Electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS)

Every EAS reaction follows the same two steps. Only the electrophile changes.

  1. The ring's π electrons attack the electrophile E+, giving a resonance-stabilized carbocation (the positive charge is shared over three ring carbons). This step is slow because aromaticity is lost.
  2. A base removes the H+ from the carbon bearing E. The ring becomes aromatic again, so the product is substituted rather than added to.

Activating, ortho/para–NH2, –NHR, –OH, –OR, –NHCOR, –R (alkyl), –C6H5Donate electrons to the ring (lone pair by resonance, or alkyl by induction)
Deactivating, ortho/para–F, –Cl, –Br, –IPull by induction (slower) but donate a lone pair by resonance (o/p)
Deactivating, meta–NO2, –CN, –SO3H, –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –COOR, –NR3+Pull electrons out of the ring (they have δ+ or + next to the ring)
There are no meta-directing activators. Every activator directs ortho/para. Halogens are the odd case: deactivating but ortho/para.
Quick test: if the atom attached to the ring has a lone pair (N, O, halogen) or is a plain alkyl, it's ortho/para. If the attached atom is double- or triple-bonded to something electronegative or carries a + charge, it's meta.

Why it works (§5.7): in ortho or para attack, one resonance form of the carbocation puts the + charge right on the carbon holding the substituent. A donor group (lone pair or alkyl) stabilizes that, so o/p wins. An electron-withdrawing group destabilizes it, so meta (which avoids that form) wins.

§5.8Oxidation and reduction of aromatic compounds

  • Side-chain oxidation: KMnO4, H2O turns any alkyl group with a benzylic H into –COOH. Toluene → benzoic acid; a propyl side chain → benzoic acid too (the extra carbons are lost). A tert-butyl group has no benzylic H, so it doesn't react.
  • Ring hydrogenation: H2 with a Pt or Rh catalyst at high pressure → cyclohexane. It is much harder than reducing an alkene, so a C=C outside the ring can be reduced while the ring stays.

§5.9Other aromatic compounds

Polycyclic aromatics are fused benzene rings: naphthalene (2 rings), anthracene and phenanthrene (3). Heterocycles have a non-carbon atom in the ring and can still be aromatic with 6 π electrons: pyridine and pyrimidine (6-membered, N in ring), pyrrole and imidazole (5-membered). These rings are in DNA bases and many drugs.

§5.10Organic synthesis: planning the order

Work backward from the target. Ask "what was the last step?" and use the directing rules to choose the order.

m-Bromonitrobenzene: nitrate first (–NO2 is a meta director), then brominate.
p-Bromonitrobenzene: brominate first (–Br is an o/p director), then nitrate and separate para from ortho.
Make m-chlorobenzoic acid from toluene.

Oxidize first: toluene + KMnO4 → benzoic acid (–COOH is a meta director). Then Cl2, FeCl3 → m-chlorobenzoic acid. If you chlorinated first, –CH3 would send the Cl ortho/para, which is wrong.

Stereochemistry at Tetrahedral Centers

Book pages 189–221 · §6.1–6.10
Why this matters: R/S assignment, counting stereoisomers and spotting meso compounds are guaranteed exam questions. Use the 3D models below until "mirror image" becomes something you can see.

§6.1–6.2Enantiomers and chirality

A molecule is chiral ("handed") if it cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, like your left and right hands. The two mirror-image forms are enantiomers.

The usual cause is a chirality center (a stereocenter): an sp3 carbon bonded to four different groups. If the molecule has a plane of symmetry, it is achiral.
Never a chirality center: any CH2 or CH3 carbon (two identical H's), and any sp2 or sp carbon. Butan-2-ol is chiral (C2 has H, OH, CH3, CH2CH3). Propan-2-ol is not, because C2 has two CH3 groups.

§6.3–6.4Optical activity

Enantiomers have identical melting points, boiling points, solubility and density. They differ in how they rotate plane-polarized light (measured with a polarimeter). One rotates it clockwise, dextrorotatory (+); the other rotates it the same amount counterclockwise, levorotatory (−).

[α]D = α / (l × C)    α = observed rotation (degrees), l = path length (dm), C = concentration (g/mL)
A 0.50 g/mL solution in a 10 cm tube rotates light +1.2°. What is [α]D?

10 cm = 1 dm, so [α]D = +1.2 / (1 × 0.50) = +2.4°.

Pasteur (1848) separated two kinds of mirror-image crystals of a tartaric acid salt by hand. They rotated light in opposite directions, which was the first evidence of enantiomers.

The (+)/(−) sign is measured in a lab. R/S comes from drawing the structure. There is no way to predict one from the other.

§6.5Assigning R and S

  1. Rank the four groups 1 to 4 with the same sequence rules as E/Z (higher atomic number first, go outward to break ties, duplicate multiple bonds).
  2. Turn the molecule so the lowest priority (4) points away from you, behind the page.
  3. Trace 1 → 2 → 3. Clockwise = R (rectus). Counterclockwise = S (sinister).
Two time-savers. (1) If group 4 points toward you (on a wedge), read the direction and then reverse it. (2) Swapping any two groups turns R into S and S into R.
Rank the groups on C2 of butan-2-ol.

–OH (O, atomic number 8) > –CH2CH3 (C with C,H,H) > –CH3 (C with H,H,H) > –H.

§6.6Diastereomers

With more than one chirality center, there are more options. A molecule with n chirality centers has up to 2n stereoisomers.

  • Enantiomers: opposite at every center (2R,3R ↔ 2S,3S).
  • Diastereomers: stereoisomers that are not mirror images, opposite at some centers and the same at others (2R,3R vs 2R,3S).
  • Diastereomers have different physical properties (melting point, solubility). Cis/trans isomers are also diastereomers.
Threonine (2 centers): 2R,3S · 2S,3R · 2R,3R · 2S,3S → 4 stereoisomers, 2 pairs of enantiomers

§6.7Meso compounds

A meso compound has chirality centers but also has an internal plane of symmetry, so it is achiral and optically inactive. Example: tartaric acid has 2 centers, so you'd expect 4 stereoisomers. But (2R,3S) and (2S,3R) are the same meso molecule, so there are only 3: (+), (−) and meso.

Look for meso when the two halves of the molecule carry the same set of groups, and the centers have opposite labels (R,S).

§6.8Racemic mixtures and resolution

A racemic mixture (racemate, written ±) is a 50:50 mix of two enantiomers. The rotations cancel, so it is optically inactive (observed rotation 0°). Reactions that make a chirality center from achiral starting materials usually give racemates.

Resolution separates a racemate. React a racemic acid with one pure enantiomer of a chiral amine. This gives two diastereomeric salts, which have different properties and can be separated (by crystallization). Then get the acids back.

§6.10Chirality in nature and chiral drugs

Biological receptors and enzymes are chiral, so they often fit only one enantiomer, the way a right glove fits only a right hand. Two enantiomers of the same drug can have different effects, which is why many drugs are now sold as a single enantiomer.
